Does the recruiter understand what this client actually needs?

Primary objective — draft: before a recruiter assesses people for a new client role, surface and help correct gaps in how they understand the client's needs and judge job-related evidence.

The owner chose to examine the evaluator's judgment behind recruitment. This is a deliberate focus, not a claim of market uniqueness or validated recruiter measurement.

What would make this worthwhile?

Hypotheses: weak submissions, missed suitable people or repeated rework may result from misread requirements or unsupported conclusions. Their frequency, cost and relevance to our fictional company still need definition.

Three diagnoses: A — requirement interpretation; B — evidence handling (including missing/conflicting information and unsupported inference); C — reference quality (unclear or faulty brief/reference). Missing information is not itself a recruiter error. Categories and dispositions are defined in knowledge/business.md. Disagreement alone does not tell us which one happened.

Working prototype boundary: a reviewed fictional case can demonstrate a model/tool/report loop. It cannot establish real recruiter competence, job performance or placement improvement.

A small illustrative scenario

Synthetic example proposed by Codex, informed by Claude's critique. Not a selected role, owner-approved reference or live AI assessment.

Fictional client priority: the new hire must investigate production incidents independently. The preferred framework is trainable.

Initial snippet: a polished résumé names that framework and says “led a migration.” It does not say who diagnosed incidents or what decisions the person owned.

Recruiter task: identify the real requirement, cite what is supported, state what remains unknown, and ask the next verification question.

Show illustrative reviewer notes — this does not grade your answers

Framework familiarity does not establish incident ownership. Missing evidence also does not establish lack of ability. Ask for a concrete incident, the person's decisions, alternatives considered and result. Seek a technical assessor where the answer exceeds the recruiter's expertise.

Second round: reveal an interviewer note of a specific, probed incident account in a different framework. This is documented candidate self-report, not independent verification. Ask whether and why the assessment changes; provisional updating while requesting technical verification is acceptable. The reference should allow multiple defensible follow-up questions.

Separate edge case: if the client brief never explains whether framework or incident responsibility matters, flag the brief for clarification rather than mark the recruiter wrong.

Q1 · Which fictional client and role should make this concrete?

Q2 · Who should act on the first report?

O3/O4 · The output needs to change a real action, not just display a score.

A · Recruiting lead, coaching a recruiter — Proposed recommendation

Benefit: Can turn a specific finding into a practice or clarification task.
Cost: Needs a usable lead summary as well as recruiter input.

B · Recruiter, for self-calibration

Benefit: Simpler individual exercise and immediate learning.
Cost: Less visibility into how a lead would use the result.

C · Client/account manager, refining the role intake

Benefit: Surfaces unclear expectations upstream.
Cost: Can shift focus from recruiter judgment to intake quality.

Q3 · Which costly judgment error should the first case expose?

O1/O2 · These are candidate problem hypotheses; we have no measured cost yet.

A · Treating a polished profile or claim as verified evidence — Proposed recommendation

Benefit: Directly tests your concern about people who look strong on paper.
Cost: A realistic example needs both a tempting claim and a clear evidence gap.

B · Rejecting a person because evidence is missing

Benefit: Tests the difference between unverified and incapable.
Cost: Needs a meaningful verification step rather than a canned yes/no.

C · Misreading what the client actually needs

Benefit: Tests role understanding before candidate comparison.
Cost: Must separate recruiter error from an unclear client brief.

Q4 · How much judgment should the first exercise capture?

O3 · Keep the first loop small and useful.

A · Two short rounds: initial evidence, then an interview reveal — Proposed recommendation

Benefit: Shows evidence use and whether new information changes the assessment appropriately.
Cost: More interaction and one more reference to review.

B · One round: assessment plus next verification question

Benefit: Fastest working slice.
Cost: Does not yet demonstrate post-interview revision.

Q5 · What should convince us the first MVP is useful?

O4/O5/O6 · Fixture correctness and business usefulness are separate questions.

A · Evidence-linked gap, defensible explanation and a concrete next action — Proposed recommendation

Benefit: You can inspect and challenge the report on a reviewed fictional case.
Cost: Qualitative demo evidence does not establish real-world effectiveness.

B · One overall recruiter score

Benefit: Easy to compare at a glance.
Cost: Can hide ambiguity and imply a validated competence measure we do not have.

C · Improved placement outcomes

Benefit: Connects directly to the business outcome.
Cost: Cannot be demonstrated honestly with a fictional first MVP.
