The assessment, as the RadHires brief states it, has no list of questions. It asks for three things, then scores them on five dimensions. For the final stage the brief says they "may walk through the submission with you", so those dimensions are the likeliest follow-up questions.

## What was requested

**The task:** invent a problem worth solving inside a business, build an AI-powered harness that solves it, and design an interface that lets an everyday, non-expert user operate it and get a polished result.

1. **Find and frame the problem.** A real, specific pain, with a crisp thesis covering:
   - who has the problem;
   - what it costs them;
   - why an AI harness fits, and honestly where it doesn't.
2. **Build the harness.** The orchestration around the model: prompts, tools, retrieval, control flow, validation, guardrails and evaluation. It must be a working vertical slice, not a diagram. Stubs are fine if you say what you stubbed and why.
3. **Design the interface** for a non-technical user:
   - what they see and click;
   - how they know it worked;
   - what happens when it doesn't;
   - why this interface suits that user.

## What you have to deliver

- **A runnable repo with a README** covering:
  - what it does and who it's for;
  - how to run it, including keys and env vars (or a recorded demo link);
  - what's real versus stubbed, and the known limitations.
- **A 1–3 page design doc (DESIGN.md)** covering:
  - the problem thesis (who, cost, why AI);
  - the end user and the interface;
  - the architecture and harness (components, failure handling, quality);
  - tooling and tradeoffs, including what you chose not to do;
  - reflections: time spent, what you'd do next, what you're least sure about.
- **Send to submissions@radhires.com** a repo link, the design doc, and any demo video or run notes. If the repo is private, share it with @joniler on GitHub.

## The five scoring dimensions

These are the likeliest follow-up questions:

1. **Problem selection and framing:** why this problem, who hurts, how much, why this approach?
2. **End-user empathy:** who is the actual user, and what is their context, vocabulary and tolerance for friction?
3. **Elegance and technical judgment:** is it right-sized, how does it fail, and how do you know it's any good (evals, checks, feedback loop)?
4. **Tooling choices and rationale:** why this stack, which tradeoffs did you weigh, and what did you deliberately leave out?
5. **Thinking and communication:** are you honest about limitations, what more time would bring, and what you're unsure of?

You also need to state how much time you actually spent.

No, the video isn't obligatory. The brief only asks for one in a specific case.

- **README:** if the project is "genuinely hard to run locally", a short recorded demo (Loom or similar) is a good substitute for run instructions. If it runs locally, you don't need one.

Your harness is meant to start with `pnpm install` and `pnpm start`, so reviewers should be able to run it themselves. The README is what's required.

A short demo is still worth making. It's your insurance if the reviewer has no API key or the app is awkward to start, and it lets them see the main flow in a couple of minutes. If you make one, keep it to 2–3 minutes on the single main workflow, and say in the README which parts are stubbed.
